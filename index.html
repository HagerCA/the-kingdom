Nobody inherits a kingdom ready-made. You build it, stone by stone, and most of the stones are boring: the agreements, the hard conversations, who does what on a Tuesday. Sometimes the boring work is the sacred work. It builds something that lasts a lifetime, and if you build it well, something you hand down. Twenty-one years in, this is how we build ours, how we leave it, and how we find our way home.

Why a kingdom

Sometimes the boring work is the sacred work

Most couples come to us talking about the big moments. The ceremony, the hard honesty, the opening of the heart, the adventure they want to have. Fair enough. Those are the moments you remember.

But the couples who lose their way almost never lost their nerve. They stopped building. Somewhere along the way the agreements went unspoken, the hard thing went unsaid, and the load one of them was carrying went unnamed for so long that it stopped being a load and started being a grudge.

"Even a crappy castle is better than no castle at all. Build it badly, and build it anyway."

A kingdom is not a five-year plan with a laminated cover. It's a life you build together, meant to last a lifetime, and if you do it well, something your children grow up inside and one day build their own from. Values you've actually paid for. Agreements you can say out loud. A way to fight that doesn't cost you the relationship. And walls strong enough, and roads clear enough, that either of you can go out exploring, in life, in travel, in friendship, and for some couples in love, and always find your way home.

Why build a kingdom at all

Building a safe kingdom lets you feel secure exploring beyond the castle walls

Nobody builds a kingdom to sit in the throne room. You build it so you can go, because you know your way home. You know the people, you know the land, and it's protected while you're out. A trip on your own. A retreat. A friendship that asks a lot of you. A new calling that takes you out of the house three nights a week. And for some couples, ours included, a season of opening the relationship to other people.

We're not going to tell you what your relationship should look like out there. We'll tell you what ours needed at home before any of it was safe.

Travel and solo trips

One of you goes. One of you holds the castle. The agreement is less about the trip and more about the call home, the kids' bedtime, and how you land again when you're back.

Callings and containers

Ceremony, men's work, women's circles, a retreat across the world. The work that makes you more yourself, and asks your partner to trust it.

Friendships that matter

Deep friendship with other men, with other women. Intimacy that is not sexual and is still intimacy, and the jealousy it can stir anyway.

Opening the relationship

Other partners, held inside agreements you both wrote. We've walked this one. We'll speak to it plainly, without names, and without telling anyone it's the right road for them.

"We use each other as armor. And then we don't know who we are without that person." The kingdom is where you walk into the castle and put the armor down, so you can get to know yourself, and your partner, without it.

What we needed at home first

We had very specific agreements, and most of the detail is ours to keep. The shape of them is worth sharing.

  • We both have to be in a good space. If we're in a fight, or the family is going through something, it isn't the time.
  • Our marriage and our children are the priority, and always will be. That had to be very, very clear.
  • Full transparency. Privacy, yes. Secrets, no. Nobody outside the marriage ever gets a "don't tell your husband I said that."
  • Zero tolerance for disrespect. Anyone who speaks against the marriage, or plays games with it, is done. That gets said up front, before any date: these are our rules, this is what we're about.

The part of me that felt unchosen

A part of me would feel unchosen, and it wasn't her responsibility to give me that self-love. So I came into a mantra: "I'm already enough. I'm already chosen. I'm already loved." I'd spend time really knowing I'm enough, then deciding to actually choose myself, then deciding to feel that love for myself. It was difficult, and extraordinarily revealing. On the hard nights, I'd do it again in the morning. It became part of my morning practice: choose me, knowing that choosing me is how I show up better for everyone else.

It took us a while to see it: three different fights were really one fight. The wound wasn't jealousy. It was exclusion.

"So many people look at us and say, 'What a perfect relationship.' It was a long road, a dogfight, to get here. But we were fighting for love. For each other, and for ourselves, all at the same time, instead of fighting with each other."

When you've lost sight of the kingdom

The way home is always the same way

Whiteouts happen, even inside your own borders. The only real mistake is to keep walking in one. Here's the road home, and it doesn't change no matter how far out you got.

  1. Acknowledge it.

    In our house, lost looks like a mood nobody names. Somebody has to say the first true sentence, and it's usually just this: "I'm sensing your energy. Something's up. We don't even need to talk about it." It doesn't need fixing yet. It only needs to be acknowledged. Then offer options, not a demand: "Do you need space? Do you need me to hold you? Or do you need me to just sit in presence with you and listen?"

  2. Stop walking.

    The urge to fix it tonight, prove your point, or leave the room is the urge to keep walking in a whiteout. Stop. Say out loud: "I think we've lost sight of the kingdom."

  3. Come back to the body.

    Nothing gets decided from a dysregulated nervous system. Breathe, walk, put a hand on your chest. When the heart is dysregulated, the voice goes up exponentially, and nobody has ever been shouted into feeling safe.

  4. Go back to the agreements.

    Not to the argument. To the thing you both said yes to when it was calm. Read it, if you wrote it down. If you never wrote it down, that's the finding.

  5. Say the first-truest sentence.

    Most stuck things are stuck because what got said was the second-truest sentence. Slow down until the first one surfaces. It's usually shorter, and it usually starts with "I'm scared that…"

  6. Decide, or decide when.

    "Not now, and we're talking about it on Sunday" is a decision. Letting it drift and calling that peace is not. Say who is doing what, out loud, so neither of you has to remember it alone.

The practice, one breath long

Before the next hard conversation, both hands on the table and one question: are we in the kingdom, or are we in a whiteout?

The kingdom is vast. You don't have to be in the castle to be home, and you can be standing side by side and still be completely lost. If you can't agree on the answer, you're in the whiteout. Start at one.

Coherence

Do it for yourself. Watch who it reaches.

Here's the part nobody tells you at the start. The work that holds a marriage together is mostly not marriage work. It's the morning you didn't skip. The regulation you did before you walked in the door. The truth you told yourself first, so the one you told your partner came out clean.

"That time is for me. The highest and best version of myself is there every single morning waiting for me. And when I get back in the house, I'm more patient. I'm a more present father and a more present partner. That's the man who comes back into the house."

Our mornings start together. Make the bed. Turn off the air conditioner. Raise the blinds. Brush our teeth, and do the toothbrush dance: both of us on one leg, flamingo-style. Then the coffee and the rest of it. And before I head outside, sometime between 6:30 and 7:30, once I've done the things I know make her life easier, I ask one sentence. Every time.

"Is there anything else you need from me before I go and take my time?"

The first year and a half, two years, I needed to get outside to feel it. I'd walk out irritated, in a rush, and I couldn't wait for the release. Ass in the grass, feet in the dirt: the prayers, the mantras, the meditations, the sun gazing, the journaling. And I'd come back in with that coherence in my body, that patience, because I was pouring into my own cup. My battery was charged by something more than sleep. It happened more times than I can count: walk out feeling like an a-hole, come back feeling like my truest and highest self.

"How do I connect to myself, and fill myself full of love and patience, so I can arrive as a more patient father, husband, partner, friend, lover? Then, the rest of the day: how do I make my wife's life the best ever? How do I make my kids' lives the best ever?"

That time alone, praying, in nature, moving, meditating, journaling, is you alone with your own nervous system. It's how you self-diagnose. When you walk back into the house, you know clearly what's yours, and you can claim it, or name it in someone else. That's how you know if you're carrying your shit or somebody else's shit. A healthy masculine makes for a healthy container, and a healthy container is where magic absolutely happens.

Coherence doesn't stay in one room. It shows up as a more patient husband, a more patient father, a son, a brother, a lover. For me the first to change, and the most, was being a partner and a husband. Then the lover, then friendship and community, and now fatherhood is following suit. And here's the thing: it's not selfish. It's selfish not to.

Couples lose sight of the kingdom when each of them waits for the other to come back first. The way out is unglamorous: one of you walks back to the castle and lights the hearth. Usually the other one sees the light in the window.
